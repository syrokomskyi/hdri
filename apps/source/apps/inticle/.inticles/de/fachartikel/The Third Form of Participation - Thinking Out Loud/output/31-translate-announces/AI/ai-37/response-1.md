In digital work, one practical problem has bothered me for a long time: we often conflate a created artifact with a useful action over time, and then wonder why models of participation, authorship, and compensation do not work. In IT, this is not an abstraction but a question of the architecture of responsibility.

- In engineering terms, there are outputs that have their own state: a component library, a design system, an audit methodology, a module, a documented structure. They can be fixed, validated against acceptance criteria, versioned, transferred, and then operated further. This is one mode of value. It is closer to an asset than to a simple expenditure of time.
- There is another mode: infrastructure support, influence on sales, operational enablement, maintaining the system’s operability. It is also critically important, but it is harder to separate from the flow of the process. This is where problems begin with attribution, rights, long-term agreements, and even with an honest description of what exactly was created.
- In practice, this means that any collaboration model in which a person ostensibly “enters through what was created” requires very strict formalism: boundaries of responsibility, written acceptance criteria, an evidentiary trail of authorship, transparent reporting, and defined exit scenarios. Without this, everything falls apart—legally, ethically, or technically.

How do you, in IT teams, distinguish a result that is a separate artifact from a result that exists only as process support?
