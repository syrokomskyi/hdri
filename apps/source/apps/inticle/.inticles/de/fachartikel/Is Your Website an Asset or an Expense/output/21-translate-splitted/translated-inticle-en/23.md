## Six conditions I consider critical

I propose six conditions that distinguish an asset website from an expense website. For now, this is my working model. Not a universal standard. But it shows which direction a project is moving in.

### 1. Measurability from day one

Without properly configured analytics, you do not know whether the website is working or not.

How many people came. Where they came from. What they did. Why they left.

If this data is not being collected and analyzed, you are managing blindly.

I have seen companies pay for "support and promotion" for years without having even basic statistics. It is like driving a car with the instrument panel covered over. You seem to be moving. But where? And how efficiently?

### 2. Full owner control

Who owns the domain? Under whose account is the hosting? Who has access to the analytics? Where is the source code?

If even one answer is "the studio" or "I don't know", then you do not own the asset. You are renting someone else's property.

I have met business owners who lost their entire website after a conflict with the studio. Because the domain had been registered in the contractor's name.

### 3. Accumulation of value

An asset website becomes stronger over time.

Mechanisms of accumulation:
- Organic traffic grows as content and authority build up.
- The email contact database increases with each visitor.
- The reputational layer (reviews, case studies, mentions) makes each next inquiry easier.

If after a year the website brings in clients only through paid advertising, i.e. a reason to review the strategy. Although for some businesses this is a deliberate choice.

### 4. Integration into operational processes

The fastest way to turn a website into an asset is to embed it into daily operations.

A website inquiry creates a task in the CRM. A payment generates documents. A consultation booking reserves a slot in the calendar.

When a website saves even a few hours a week on routine work, i.e. tangible value. Even if it is not yet bringing in new clients.

For a small business, where the owner is often both secretary and sales manager, operational integration is the first thing that shows a return on investment.

### 5. Lifecycle support

Launching a website is not the end of the project. It is the beginning.

Without regular updates and optimization, any website degrades. Search algorithms change. Technologies become outdated. New security requirements appear.

I know companies whose websites, after two years without support, went from being niche leaders to technical junk. It is easier to rebuild them from scratch than to fix them. Especially now, in the era of artificial intelligence (AI).

### 6. Machine readability

A relatively new condition. It is becoming critical.

The share of queries handled by AI systems is growing. ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity answer questions based on info from websites.

If your website is not structured for machine reading-there is no schema.org markup, prices are embedded in images, contact details are unclear-your business becomes invisible in this channel.

How quickly this will happen is unclear. But the trend is visible. Ignoring it is short-sighted.
