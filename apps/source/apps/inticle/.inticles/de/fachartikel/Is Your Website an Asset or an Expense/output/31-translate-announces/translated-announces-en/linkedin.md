Most B2B websites look like an investment.
In reality, they behave like an expense.

The problem is usually not the design and not the choice of CMS.
The problem lies in the project's underlying logic.

If a website is bought as a digital object, the result is a storefront.
If it is designed as a system, there is a chance to accumulate value.

In this article, I examine a simple test criterion: what happens if paid acquisition stops tomorrow.

Then come six architectural conditions without which a website can hardly be called an asset:
measurability, control over rights and access, accumulation of effect, integration into operational work, lifecycle support, and machine readability.

I also define the boundary of responsibility.
An agency can be responsible for infrastructure, analytics, doc, and the handover of access credentials.
But it cannot honestly promise an economic outcome if it does not control the offer, the traffic, and lead handling.

For the Mittelstand, this is also a question of continuity.
The less a business depends on the personality of the owner, the greater its digital resilience.

A new layer is now being added to this: a website must be understandable not only to people, but also to machine systems.
This is no longer a decorative requirement, but part of the infrastructure of visibility.

It seems useful to me to look at a website not as a launch project, but as a manageable business system.

If you apply this perspective to your website today, does it look more like an asset or more like an expense?
