# Is Your Website an Asset or an Expense? One Question That Changes Everything

Over twenty-five years of work, I have seen hundreds of websites costing from thousands to hundreds of thousands of euros. Technologically sophisticated. With thoughtful UX. With animations.

Most of them were pure expenses.

Not because they were "badly built". But because they were bought as beautiful objects, not as working systems.

Recently, I started thinking about what separates an "expense website" from an "asset website". Why do most business owners pay for the first while thinking (imagining) they are buying the second?

## Asset or expense: what is the difference for your business

In accounting, an asset is a resource under your control from which future economic benefits are expected. But for the practical discussion here, I use a more direct definition.

An **asset** is a system that becomes stronger over time. Stop advertising budgets tomorrow, and the asset will keep working. Maybe not as intensively. But it will keep working.

An **expense** requires constant investment without accumulating effect. Stop the payments, and the effect disappears.

This is, of course, a simplification. A website may have a branding function, operational value, a contact base-even if organic traffic is not growing. But as a first test, the question works: turn off all advertising tomorrow-what remains afterward?

If the answer is "nothing" or "a nice online brochure", it is worth thinking about.

## Six conditions I consider critical

I propose six conditions that distinguish an asset website from an expense website. At this point, this is my working model. Not a universal standard. But it shows which direction a project is moving in.

### 1. Measurability from day one

Without properly configured analytics, you do not know whether the website is working or not.

How many people came. Where they came from. What they did. Why they left.

If this data is not being collected and analyzed, you are managing blind.

I have seen companies pay for "support and promotion" for years without having basic statistics. It is like driving a car with the dashboard covered up. You seem to be moving. But where to? How efficiently?

### 2. Full owner control

Who owns the domain? Under whose account is the hosting? Who has access to analytics? Where is the source code?

If even one answer is "the studio" or "I don't know", then you do not own the asset. You are renting someone else's property.

I have met business owners who lost their website entirely after a conflict with the studio-because the domain had been registered in the contractor's name.

### 3. Value accumulation

An asset website becomes stronger over time.

Mechanisms of accumulation:
- Organic traffic grows as content and authority accumulate.
- The email contact base increases with every visitor.
- The reputational layer (reviews, case studies, mentions) makes each next inquiry easier.

If after a year the website brings in clients only through paid advertising, i.e. a reason to review the strategy. Although for some businesses, this is a conscious choice.

### 4. Integration into operational processes

The fastest way to turn a website into an asset is to embed it into daily operations.

A website inquiry creates a task in the CRM. A payment generates documents. A consultation booking reserves a slot in the calendar.

When a website saves even a few hours a week on routine tasks, i.e. tangible value-even if it is not yet bringing in new clients.

For small businesses, where the owner is often both secretary and sales manager, operational integration is usually the first place where the return on investment becomes visible.

### 5. Lifecycle support

Launching a website is not the end of the project. It is the beginning.

Without regular updates and optimization, any website degrades. Search algorithms change. Technologies become outdated. New security requirements appear.

I know companies whose websites, after two years without support, went from niche leaders to technical junk. It is easier to rebuild them from scratch than to fix them. Especially now, in the age of artificial intelligence (AI).

### 6. Machine readability

A relatively new condition. It is becoming critical.

The share of queries processed by AI systems is growing. ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity answer questions based on info from websites.

If your website is not structured for machine reading-no schema.org markup, prices embedded in images, unclear contact details-your business becomes invisible in this channel.

How quickly this will happen is unclear. But the trend is visible. Ignoring it is shortsighted.

## Area of responsibility: what a studio can do, and what depends on you

Let me say something unpopular among colleagues.

A studio that promises a specific number of leads or ROI from a website either does not understand what it is talking about or is deliberately misleading you.

Website ROI depends on four variables:
1/ The quality of the website itself (studio).
2/ The volume and quality of traffic (advertising, SEO, reputation).
3/ The attractiveness of the offer (product, price, terms).
4/ The quality of lead handling (speed, professionalism).

The studio controls one quarter of the equation. Promising results while controlling none of the other 75% of the variables is either naïve or manipulative.

What a studio can guarantee:
- Technical reliability and speed.
- Properly configured analytics.
- Transfer of ownership rights and access.
- Doc and training.
- Regular support according to a defined process.

Everything else is a shared area of responsibility.

## Continuity and digital independence

For the German SME sector, there is a special aspect.

Many craft businesses and family-owned companies face the challenge of business succession. What discourages potential successors? *Inhaberrisiko*-dependence on the owner as a person.

A company where clients come through the owner's personal relationships is valued lower than a company with established acquisition channels. A functioning website with a measurable funnel is a way to demonstrate business independence. This directly affects valuation during succession.

On top of that, there is GDPR and other requirements. A website that creates legal risks is not simply an ineffective expense. It is a potential threat. Proper privacy config is not a formality, but basic hygiene.

## AI and the new rules of the game: what is already changing

If you think conversations about AI are hype that does not concern you, look more closely.

Already today, a noticeable share of people search for info not through Google. They ask Perplexity or ChatGPT.

What does this mean for your website?

AI systems need to understand: what you do, where you are located, how much your services cost, and how to contact you. If the info is unstructured, hidden in PDFs, or embedded in images, then for AI, you do not exist.

Tomorrow, not everyone will stop using traditional search. But ignoring the trend means missing a growing channel. Especially if competitors are already adapting.

## When you do not need an asset website

Let me say something you rarely hear from people in the web industry.

Not everyone needs an asset website right now.

If the business gets enough clients through personal recommendations, does not plan to scale in the next year, operates in a narrow niche where everyone knows each other, then a simple brochure website is enough.

Seriously. There is no point in building a complex system where it will not be used.

But (there is always a "but") it is important to understand the transition point. When you want to move beyond personal contacts. Reach a new audience. Reduce dependence on personal involvement. Prepare the business for succession…

I.e. when the difference between an "expense website" and an "asset website" becomes critical.

## In the end

I am not calling on anyone to urgently rebuild their website or order expensive audits.

I am suggesting that you answer a few questions honestly:

- Do you know how many people come to your website and what they do there?
- Could you move the website to another contractor tomorrow?
- Does the website become more effective each month, or does it stop working without advertising?
- Does the website save time on routine work?
- Are the content and technologies being updated?
- Is the structure of the business clear not only to people, but also to the search systems of the future?

The answers will show where you are. It is normal if not all conditions are met. What matters is understanding what you are buying and what you expect from it.

The problem begins when you think you are investing in an asset, while in reality you are financing… simply a storefront.

The difference is not in the price or the technologies. The difference is in the approach. Are you buying an object or building a system?

Most websites remain expenses not because they are badly made. But because from the start they were conceived as artifacts, not as tools.

Change the approach. The same budget will produce a different result.

For the German *Mittelstand* (small and medium-sized businesses) with its focus on long-term resilience, this is natural logic. Transfer it from production processes into digital infrastructure.

A website is not a one-time purchase. It is a system that requires attention, development, and a clear understanding of what it is for. Do you have that?

## Check in 3 minutes: is your website closer to an asset or an expense?

If you want to turn this idea from reflection into practice, take a short self-audit based on these same six conditions. At the end, you will get not a "score for the sake of a score", but a risk profile: what is already working, where the website is vulnerable, and which next steps make sense.
