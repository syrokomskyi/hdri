In small agencies and studios, one problem keeps recurring: value is created by specialists’ hands, but once the project is finished, almost everything reverts to billing for time. If you look at this operationally, an uncomfortable question arises: is it possible to build a form of participation in which the contribution does not disappear when the task is closed?

- A useful framework, in my view, is to distinguish between two types of contribution. The first is a completed artifact that can be described, accepted against criteria, resold, or used as part of the studio’s infrastructure. The second is an effect over time: support, sales, acquisition, coordination. For agency processes, this is critical, because it is precisely the mixing of these two types that creates most conflicts around a “fair share.”
- If we talk about the operating model in a mature way, then without written acceptance criteria, documentation of authorship, transparent reporting, and predefined exit scenarios, such a system does not work. It quickly begins to disguise unpaid or underpaid labor as a beautiful idea of partnership. Here, ethics and operational design are not separable at all.
- The most difficult point is risk asymmetry. The studio provides infrastructure, sales, and the management framework. The author invests time without a guaranteed outcome. If revenue is delayed or does not materialize at all, the model begins to select not the strongest, but those who can afford to wait. In an agency environment, this is no longer philosophy, but a question of whom the system actually allows to participate.

What do you think: for agencies, is a participation model built around the created artifact more viable, or in a service business does this almost inevitably slide into attribution conflicts?
